\begin{abstract}
Looking several splits ahead can improve a decision tree, but the search quickly becomes expensive. Does every tree in a forest need this extra computation? We study mixed forests in which a minority of members optimize their splits over a longer horizon. Repeated paired experiments on PMLB classification tasks compare unpruned trees and forests with matching depth limits and common hyperparameters. Replacing a small fraction of greedy members improves mean accuracy in shallow forests under both feature policies studied. Some mixtures also achieve higher mean accuracy with less fitting work than much larger greedy forests. These gains diminish as the permitted final depth increases, while uniformly farther-sighted induction remains costly. In a separate experiment, training-only tuning of both conventional and mixed-capable families gives the expanded family a higher benchmark-mean accuracy, at substantially greater selection and refit costs. The findings identify selective use of farther-sighted members as a useful option for shallow ensembles and show why split-search depth, tree capacity, and model-selection cost should be evaluated together.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Decision trees make a sequence of decisions, yet CART chooses each split by its immediate reduction in impurity \cite{breiman1984cart}. A split that looks unhelpful on its own may reveal a useful interaction after another split. Searching several generations ahead can find such opportunities, but doing so at every node of every tree is expensive. For a forest, this raises a different question: \emph{can a few farther-sighted trees improve the ensemble without paying for farther sight throughout?}

We call a tree $k$-sighted when it scores a candidate split through the best local subtree of up to $k$ split generations, including the current one. A mixed forest combines members with different horizons. We evaluate this well-studied split-search principle as a way to allocate computation within an ensemble. Two comparisons are kept separate: fixed architectures isolate the effect of replacing greedy members, while training-only tuning compares conventional and mixed-capable model families fairly.

Shallow forests are a natural starting point. Limiting member size can regularize an ensemble, and smaller trees can be beneficial in noisy settings \cite{zhou2023trees,duroux2018impact}. A depth-three member has at most eight leaves, but a forest of such members can define many more prediction regions. We therefore study shallow ensembles alongside depth-six and unrestricted controls, keeping total depth and the other common settings matched between greedy and farther-sighted learners.

The experiments reveal three useful patterns. First, replacing a minority of greedy members improves shallow-forest mean accuracy under both feature policies studied. Second, a small mixed forest can outperform a much larger greedy forest while using less measured fitting work. Third, allowing farther-sighted candidates in training-only model selection increases benchmark-mean accuracy, although the search for those candidates is much more costly. Increasing every member's horizon is therefore a different proposition from using farther sight selectively. The depth comparisons further show that the benefits are strongest when tree capacity is constrained.

The study uses repeated paired partitions of 57 PMLB classification tasks and reports predictive differences, uncertainty, fitting work, and selection cost. Code and a guide to reproducing the results are available at \url{https://github.com/MathiasValla/non-greedy-decision-trees}.

\section{Related work}
Greedy induction and bootstrap aggregation provide the foundations for the models studied here \cite{breiman1984cart,quinlan1986induction,breiman1996bagging}. Random forests additionally randomize the features available at each split \cite{breiman2001random}.

Non-greedy induction predates the present study. Norton's overlapping search commits the shallowest choice and searches again below it \cite{norton1989generating}, while Ragavan and Rendell \cite{ragavan1993lookahead} used lookahead for feature construction. Murthy and Salzberg's LOOK procedure scores a split with optimally split children, corresponding to the two-generation case here \cite{murthy1995lookahead}. Their experiments already question whether added computation reliably improves prediction. Later work revisited pathology \cite{elomaa2005lookahead}. Esmeir and Markovitch \cite{esmeir2007anytime} studied anytime induction, including bagged non-greedy learners and the tradeoff between computation per tree and committee size. Norouzi et al. \cite{norouzi2015efficient} jointly optimize oblique split and leaf parameters, a different form of non-greediness.

A direct forest precedent is the stepwise lookahead forest of Donick and Lera \cite{donick2021stepwise}, which optimizes two-level blocks to recover interactions in noisy data. Our builder commits one root split and repeats the search at each child; the ensemble question is how to distribute horizons across members. Randomized splits \cite{geurts2006extremely} and random final depths \cite{horn2026randomdepth} offer other ways to vary members. The relationship between ensemble diversity and accuracy has itself been studied extensively \cite{kuncheva2003diversity}. Here we measure the effect of changing horizon allocation directly.

Modern optimal-tree methods exploit mathematical programming \cite{bertsimas2017optimal,verwer2019learning,aghaei2025strong}, bounds and sparse objectives \cite{hu2019optimal,lin2020generalized}, and cached or separable subproblems in DL8.5, MurTree, and STreeD \cite{aglin2020caching,demirovic2022murtree,vanderlinden2023separable}. Reference ensembles can guide restricted search \cite{mctavish2022reference}. SPLIT and LicketySPLIT combine non-greedy choices with greedy continuation \cite{babbar2025nearoptimal}, using objectives and continuation rules different from the truncated terminal impurity considered here. Recent comparisons further show how objectives, size constraints, and tuning affect the relative performance of greedy and optimal trees \cite{vanderlinden2026optimalgreedy}. These developments place our contribution in empirical evaluation of horizon allocation, rather than in proposing another optimal-tree solver.

\subsection{Tree-size regularization}
Tree size is also a regularization choice. Zhou and Mentch \cite{zhou2023trees} report benefits from smaller random-forest members at low signal-to-noise ratios. Duroux and Scornet \cite{duroux2018impact} study subsampling and early termination theoretically for quantile-split regression forests and empirically for Breiman-style regression forests. These regression studies vary leaf limits and resampling; Duroux and Scornet's small-tree empirical alternative also removes bootstrap resampling. They motivate studying constrained member size, rather than prescribing a universal classification-tree depth. Competitive small classification trees \cite{bertsimas2017optimal} provide a related motivation. At the other end of the spectrum, consistency results for random forests study trees whose size can grow with the sample \cite{scornet2015consistency}. Our comparison of shallow, deeper, and unrestricted bootstrap Gini classifiers examines how the benefit of split optimization changes with capacity.

\section{Method}
\subsection{Horizon and final depth}
Let $S$ be the examples reaching a node, $I(S)$ its Gini impurity, and $W(S)$ its total weight. Let $D$ be the maximum number of splits along a final root-to-leaf path and $d$ the current depth. At that node the actual search horizon is $h=\min(k,D-d)$; when $D$ is unrestricted, $h=k$. Increasing $k$ changes search, whereas increasing $D$ changes capacity.

Define the optimal local terminal score recursively:
\begin{align}
 C_0(S)&=W(S)I(S),\\
 C_h(S)&=\min\left\{C_0(S),\min_{s\in\mathcal S(S)}
 \big[C_{h-1}(S_l(s))+C_{h-1}(S_r(s))\big]\right\}.
 \label{eq:recursion}
\end{align}
Here $\mathcal S(S)$ contains admissible axis-aligned midpoint thresholds between distinct observed feature values, subject to the leaf, depth, and feature constraints. The selected root minimizes the second term when it is no worse than stopping, up to numerical tolerance. Feasible zero-gain splits are allowed, as in unpruned CART. Only that root is committed. The configured horizon is then restored at each actual child. This is receding-horizon induction, not a single depth-$k$ fit followed by an unchanged completion.

For a fixed node, expanding the feasible horizon cannot worsen the optimal terminal training impurity. With all features and $k\geq D$ at the root, the search covers the depth-bounded terminal-impurity problem; with feature subsampling it is conditional on the seeded feature policy. This makes $k=D=3$ an instructive full-horizon case. The experiments ask how these local training improvements translate into prediction and cost.

\subsection{Matched trees and mixed forests}
The one-sighted baseline is scikit-learn CART. All learners use Gini impurity, the same permitted total depth, split and leaf minima, and feature policy. None is pruned, and feasible zero-gain splits are allowed. Every distinct admissible numerical threshold is considered; there is no candidate cap. At each node we consider either all $p$ features or an initially sampled subset of $\max(1,\lfloor\sqrt p\rfloor)$ features, examining more if that subset provides no admissible split.

A mixed forest replaces designated CART members with farther-sighted trees, using the same bootstrap draw and seed for each replacement. Every ensemble position contains exactly one member. Bootstrap multiplicities are sample weights, and the sample-count constraints exclude zero-weight rows. Horizons are assigned without using class labels, in blocks of 20 so that every evaluated ensemble size has the specified composition. Predictions average class-aligned member probabilities. The all-greedy construction was checked against the library random forest over the common parameter grid.

We describe a mixture by its member proportions and, for convenience, its mean horizon $\bar k=\sum_j p_j k_j$. The proportions matter: $90\%$ one-sighted plus $10\%$ two-sighted and $95\%$ one-sighted plus $5\%$ three-sighted both have $\bar k=1.1$, but involve different searches.

\subsection{Search cost}
The two child optima in Eq.~\eqref{eq:recursion} can be computed separately. If every recursive call has at most $m$ candidates and $E_h$ counts candidate evaluations, a worst-case bound is
\begin{equation}
 E_0=0,\qquad E_h\leq m+2m E_{h-1},\qquad
 E_h\leq m\frac{(2m)^h-1}{2m-1}.
\end{equation}
The bound is exponential in local horizon. Final fitting sums such searches over the committed nodes, with actual work also depending on sample sizes and stopping. A fixed short horizon can therefore build a deep tree; increasing the final-depth limit does not itself increase $k$.

\section{Experimental protocol}
\subsection{Dataset sample and repeated partitions}
We use a tractable 57-dataset sample of PMLB classification tasks \cite{olson2017pmlb}. All methods share PMLB's supplied numeric representation and categorical encodings. A common complete-case rule removes eleven rows from \texttt{penguins}; no imputation or scaling is applied. Dataset names, dimensions, class counts, and preprocessing records are available with the code.

Each dataset receives five paired stratified $75/25$ outer train--test partitions with fixed, reproducible seeds. The seeds differ between repetitions, as do estimator and bootstrap seeds. All methods share the same partitions within a repetition.

\subsection{Models and training-only selection}
Fixed-architecture comparisons use $D\in\{3,6,\infty\}$, minimum leaf size one, and either all features or square-root feature subsampling. Single trees use $k=1,2,3$ with identical common settings and no pruning. Every forest composition is evaluated at $T\in\{20,40,60,100,200\}$. In addition to the three pure horizons, each pair $(1,2)$, $(1,3)$, and $(2,3)$ uses $5\%,10\%,25\%,$ or $50\%$ of the farther-sighted horizon. A sparse three-way composition uses $85\%,10\%,5\%$ of horizons one, two, and three. Thus sixteen explicitly specified compositions span every ensemble size; equal average horizons do not identify identical mixtures.

In the separate tuned experiment, three model families receive training-only inner cross-validation. The conventional greedy family (RF) contains only CART members. Its feature setting is tuned: a random subset at each split gives a feature-subsampled random forest, while using all features gives bagged CART. The 1/2 family allows pure forests and mixtures of one- and two-sighted members. The inclusive 1/2/3 family allows the full sixteen-composition grid. Thus both expanded families can also select a conventional greedy forest.

All three families share depths $\{3,6,\infty\}$, leaf minima $\{1,5\}$, the two feature policies, and ensemble sizes $\{20,40,60,100\}$. They have 48, 288, and 768 candidate configurations, respectively. The 200-member models appear only in the fixed experiment. Training-only selection keeps the outer test data separate from hyperparameter choice \cite{cawley2010selection}.

Inner folds are shared across families. Shuffled stratified cross-validation uses $\min(3,c_{\min})$ folds, where $c_{\min}$ is the smallest observed outer-training class count. A singleton or single-class training partition instead uses ordinary shuffled three-fold cross-validation. Mean inner accuracy chooses the model; ties favour lower measured member-fitting work, then a fixed candidate ordering. The chosen configurations are refitted separately on the outer-training data before test evaluation. Three-sighted members are included in both fixed and tuned experiments.

\subsection{Timing and outcomes}
Accuracy is the primary outcome; balanced accuracy, the mean recall over classes present in the scoring partition, assesses sensitivity to class imbalance. Scores and costs are first averaged over repetitions within each dataset, then equally across datasets.

For fixed forests, fitting work is the sum of measured fitting durations of the included members. Fixed single-tree timings likewise cover fitting alone. Both exclude construction, bootstrap preparation, and prediction. For tuned models, we report the selected model's direct refit wall time, including construction and bootstrap preparation, separately from cross-validation fitting work. The latter includes the tree fits needed to evaluate the whole candidate grid, even when a cheap model is ultimately selected. Reused fits are counted once within each family's search, and each family is charged the work it would require if run on its own. Recorded workflow-stage wall times are also supplied; they exclude checkpoint input/output, startup, downloads, and idle gaps.

Experiments use five concurrent, single-threaded workers on a MacBook Air with an eight-core Apple M3 processor and 16 GB memory. Numerical figures are computed from experimental results using reproducible NumPy, SciPy, pandas, and Matplotlib workflows. OpenAI Codex (OpenAI) assisted with plotting code, as disclosed below; no generative image model was used.

\subsection{Uncertainty and multiple comparisons}
For each contrast, paired repetition differences are averaged within datasets. A percentile bootstrap with 20,000 resamples of the dataset means gives a pointwise, unadjusted $95\%$ interval for the mean effect. Two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests compare these same dataset-level differences \cite{demsar2006comparisons}; our calculation omits zero differences. Mean-effect intervals and signed-rank tests describe different aspects of the effect distribution: its average and its location under the usual symmetry assumption.

One Holm adjustment \cite{holm1979sequential} covers eleven primary contrasts: eight fixed 100-member comparisons of $10\%$ two- or three-sighted replacements with matched greedy forests, at depth three or unrestricted depth and either feature setting; and three pairwise comparisons of the tuned families. We denote the adjusted values by $p_H$. Other depths, sizes, proportions, and single-tree contrasts are descriptive.

Two sensitivity analyses change benchmark weighting or membership. One gives equal weight to 48 known task families, averaging related GAMETES, MONK, LED, parity, and Cy Young tasks first. The other removes 19 explicitly named synthetic/constructed tasks, leaving 38 tasks. The full membership lists are supplied with the results.

\subsection{Sparse horizon replacements}
At $T=100$, $D=3$, with all features, replacing ten CART members with
horizon-two or horizon-three members gives mean gains of $1.06$ points
[$0.45$, $1.77$] and $1.27$ points [$0.61$, $2.02$]. Both medians
are zero; wins/ties/losses are $27/21/9$ and $28/14/15$.
Their joint-Holm signed-rank values are $p_H=0.00996$ and $0.02066$.
Balanced-accuracy gains are $1.15/1.33$ points. Mean member-fitting

Figure~\ref{fig:tradeoff} shows why mixing is worth considering.
A 20-member forest with five two-sighted trees and fifteen CART trees
has $74.06\%$ mean accuracy at $0.0955$ s of fitting work. The
200-member pure CART forest has $73.02\%$ accuracy at $0.1338$ s.
In this example, replacing a few members is more effective than
adding many greedy trees: mean accuracy is $1.04$ points higher
with about $29\%$ less fitting work. The 20-member mixture with
only two two-sighted members reaches $73.37\%$ mean accuracy at
$0.0470$ s of fitting work, also exceeding the 200-member greedy
forest at lower work.

Mixtures occupy intermediate positions between the cheap greedy
curve and the expensive uniformly farther-sighted curves. A
100-member forest with $25\%$ two-sighted members reaches
$74.57\%$ at $0.4709$ s. Smaller pure farther-sighted forests
are competitive too: below the $0.7$ s mean-work reference, the

\section{Discussion}
% BEGIN GENERATED discussion_text
% Author-comment revision, 10 October 2026.

The main practical finding is that farther sight need not be an all-or-nothing
choice. Sparse replacements improve shallow-forest mean accuracy under both
feature policies, and three of the four primary shallow contrasts retain
$p_H<0.05$. Figure~\ref{fig:tradeoff} shows the complementary computation
argument: adding a few two-sighted members can achieve more than adding many
greedy members. The benefit of a mixed forest is the option to spend more
search effort on only part of the ensemble.

The tuned comparison strengthens the predictive finding under the original
benchmark weighting. The inclusive
family's mean-effect interval lies above zero even though the conventional
baseline is tuned on the same common parameter grid. Its higher mean
accuracy is therefore not simply an artefact of comparing tuned mixtures
with an untuned RF. Selection often chooses an actual mixture, but also
chooses pure forests. The tuned result concerns the value of the expanded
family; the fixed comparisons isolate the effect of replacing specified
greedy members with farther-sighted members.

The depth controls indicate where the observed benefits are strongest. When each
member has few splits available, selecting those splits more carefully can
help. With more splits available, greedy induction may recover useful
partitions later in the tree. Fixed greedy forests improve at deeper
capacities, and tuned RF selects depth six or unrestricted depth in 219 of
285 outer dataset--repetition selections. The unrestricted all-feature farther-sighted trees grow deeper
yet predict less accurately, a pattern consistent with overfitting.
Optimizing local terminal impurity does not penalize tree size; a more
thorough search can therefore fit sample-specific structure. This is a
plausible explanation to test with training--validation curves and explicit
regularization. Feature-subsampled forests retain small positive mean gains
at unrestricted depth, so the feature policy also matters.

\subsection{Scope and limitations}
The gains are heterogeneous. For tuned inclusive versus RF, the mean
gain is positive but the median is zero and the adjusted signed-rank test
does not reject its location null. Also, we note that a nonsignificant
test does not show that two methods perform equally. The inclusive mean
gain becomes $1.03$ points [$-0.27$, $2.90$] under equal weighting of
48 known task families and $0.53$ [$-0.91$, $2.77$] on the 38 tasks
outside the named synthetic/constructed set. The corresponding 1/2
effects are $0.61$ [$-0.32$, $2.02$] and $0.38$ [$-0.66$, $2.09$].
These sensitivities show how the benchmark's composition affects the
estimated average benefit. The sample is rather small and includes related
and constructed tasks; overlapping holdouts are averaged within tasks,
not treated as additional independent datasets.

Computation is the other important limit. Three-sighted search and full
selection over expanded families are expensive. The common hyperparameter
grid ensures matched choices, but the mixed families have more candidates
to evaluate. Figure~\ref{fig:tradeoff} compares benchmark means: the
illustrated mixtures beat particular greedy forests at lower measured
fitting work, not necessarily the best pure forest at every budget.
Connecting plotted points does not evaluate an intermediate model, and
the curve excludes selection work. A per-dataset fixed-budget policy
would need training-only selection and a check that the final fit respects
the budget. The timings also reflect this implementation and concurrent
hardware load, not an intrinsic cost of every non-greedy method. Larger
independent tasks and comparisons with efficient bounded or hybrid search
\cite{aglin2020caching,demirovic2022murtree,vanderlinden2023separable,babbar2025nearoptimal}
would establish how far the observed tradeoffs extend.
% END GENERATED discussion_text

\section{Conclusion}
% BEGIN GENERATED conclusion_text
% Author-comment revision, 10 October 2026.
Across the tested depth-three settings, sparse replacements consistently
yielded higher observed benchmark-mean accuracy than their matched greedy
controls. They also offer a useful
accuracy--fitting-work compromise: a small mixture can outperform a much
larger greedy forest while requiring less member-fitting work. Allowing
farther-sighted candidates in training-only tuning with matched common
hyperparameters raises
benchmark-mean accuracy as well, although selection is substantially more
expensive. Together, these results support allocating farther sight
selectively rather than assuming that every member should search farther.
Larger independent, resource-constrained comparisons with modern search
methods are needed for a more general conclusion. This study provides a
concrete empirical step: mixed forests can improve on larger pure greedy
forests at lower benchmark-mean fitting work.
% END GENERATED conclusion_text
